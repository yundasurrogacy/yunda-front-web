Short answer: A known egg donor is someone whose identity and relationship to you are established from the start; an “anonymous” donor is usually recruited through a clinic or donor program without an existing personal relationship. Neither route is automatically better for gay men. The right fit depends on your comfort with future contact, the donor’s boundaries, clinic screening, genetic information, legal agreements, timing, budget categories, and the needs of a future child. Also, “anonymous” may not mean permanently unidentifiable: programs may instead use terms such as nonidentified or identity-release, and consumer DNA databases can change practical expectations of privacy. Before IVF, ask the clinic exactly what donor category it uses, what information can be shared now and later, and how screening is documented. Then ask an independent reproductive-law attorney how parentage, consent, disclosure, records, and cross-border issues apply to your case. Known, nonidentified, and identity-release donors: what changes?

The labels describe how the donor and intended parents connect and what identity information may be available—not the quality of the donor or the likelihood of a successful IVF cycle. A known donor may be a friend, acquaintance, or relative. A nonidentified donor is generally selected through a program without direct identifying information being exchanged at the outset. An identity-release donor may agree that specified identifying information can become available to a donor-conceived person under the program’s policy, often at a defined age.

Terminology and record-access rules vary. Ask for the program’s written definition instead of relying on the word “anonymous.” If a known donor is a relative, clinics may apply additional counseling and review. The American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) discusses screening and counseling for gamete donation and separately addresses the ethical issues that can arise in intrafamilial donation.

Medical and genetic screening questions

How you met the donor does not replace clinical screening. ASRM’s donation guidance describes medical history, physical assessment, infectious-disease testing, genetic considerations, and psychoeducational counseling as important parts of donor evaluation. Your reproductive endocrinologist and donor program should explain which requirements apply to the planned cycle.

  • Which screening steps are required for this donor and this clinic?
  • When were infectious-disease tests and genetic assessments performed?
  • How will the donor’s results be compared with the intended father providing sperm?
  • Who explains residual genetic risk and recommends genetic counseling?
  • How are significant medical-history updates communicated after donation?
  • What happens if the receiving clinic does not accept an outside program’s documentation?

Legal agreements, privacy, and future contact

Known donation needs clear expectations precisely because the people may already care about one another. Discuss communication during treatment, social roles after birth, information sharing, expenses or compensation, handling of stored eggs or embryos, and what happens if someone’s circumstances change. Each party may need independent legal advice so that the agreement is informed and does not blur the donor’s role with legal parentage.

With a nonidentified or identity-release donor, review the program’s policy rather than assuming there will never be contact. Ask who keeps records, whether the donor can update medical history, what a donor-conceived person may request in the future, and how direct-to-consumer DNA testing affects expectations. A reproductive-law attorney should advise on your jurisdictions; no webpage or donor-program label can guarantee lifelong anonymity.

Planning for two fathers and a future child

Some couples decide early which intended father will provide sperm; others ask the clinic about creating embryos from each partner. That choice can affect screening, laboratory planning, embryo records, and later family conversations. It does not change the need for accurate donor information or thoughtful disclosure planning.

Consider these questions together:

  • What story do we want to tell our child about the donor and how our family was built?
  • What donor information would we want available for future health questions?
  • If we know the donor, what words will everyone use for their role?
  • If identity release is possible, how will we prepare for future contact?
  • How will we keep records accessible as our child grows?

Extra questions for international intended parents

Cross-border plans can add document, travel, transport, and parentage questions. Before choosing a donor program, ask whether the clinic will accept its records and materials, which documents need translation or authentication, how eggs or embryos would be transported if necessary, and which professionals are responsible for each handoff. Separately, obtain legal advice in every relevant jurisdiction about parentage, citizenship, travel documents, and the use of donor information. Requirements can change and depend on individual facts.

A practical sequence for making the decision

  1. Define your family preferences. Discuss relationship boundaries, desired information, disclosure, and future contact before comparing profiles.
  2. Confirm clinic acceptance. Ask what donor categories, screening records, agencies, banks, and outside materials your IVF clinic will accept.
  3. Compare complete pathways. Review all likely steps and cost categories, not only the initial donor fee or one quoted timeline.
  4. Schedule counseling and legal review. Use qualified professionals who understand donor conception, LGBTQ+ families, and the jurisdictions involved.
  5. Document decisions and handoffs. Keep a shared checklist for consent, screening, legal clearance, records, transport, IVF, and later updates.

Frequently asked questions

Is a known egg donor less medically screened?

Not by definition. The clinic determines the medical, infectious-disease, genetic, and counseling requirements for the planned donation. Ask the clinic to explain and document its protocol rather than assuming that an existing relationship changes the clinical standard.

Does anonymous egg donation guarantee privacy forever?

No absolute guarantee should be assumed. Program terms, laws, record policies, identity-release provisions, and consumer DNA matching can all affect future identifiability. Ask for the written policy and obtain legal advice about your circumstances.

Can a friend or family member be an egg donor?

It may be possible, subject to clinic requirements and applicable law. Intrafamilial donation can require added attention to voluntariness, family dynamics, counseling, genetics, and independent legal advice. The clinic and attorneys must assess the specific arrangement.

Which option is faster or less expensive?

Neither option is always faster or less expensive. A known donor may require coordination, screening, counseling, and legal work; a donor program may add availability, program, transport, or storage steps. Compare a written end-to-end pathway for your actual clinic and jurisdictions.
